# Customer-Winback-Model
Predictive model to identify inactive customers with a high probability of reactivation using WOE and logistic regression.

## Predicting Organic Customer Reactivation

### Overview

Financial institutions invest significant resources in acquiring and retaining customers. However, customers who become inactive represent a different opportunity: some may naturally return to the institution, while others are unlikely to reactivate without a targeted intervention.

This project developed a predictive model to identify former customers with a high probability of returning to an active relationship with the institution.

The model was developed using historical customer information and evaluated based on observed customer reactivation behavior.

> **Important:** The model predicts **organic winback propensity**. No targeted marketing or retention campaign was applied to the customers classified by the model. Therefore, the observed reactivation rate should not be interpreted as a causal effect generated by the model.

---

## 1. Business Problem

The institution had a large population of customers who had previously been active but subsequently became inactive.

The business challenge was to identify which inactive customers had a higher probability of returning to the institution.

Instead of treating all inactive customers equally, the objective was to develop a predictive model capable of ranking customers according to their likelihood of reactivation.

This ranking could eventually support future winback strategies by allowing the institution to prioritize customers with the highest predicted propensity.

---

## 2. Objective

The primary objective was to develop a binary classification model that estimates:

$$
P(\text{Winback}=1 \mid X)
$$

where:

* **Winback = 1:** the customer returned to an active relationship.
* **Winback = 0:** the customer remained inactive during the observation period.
* (X): customer characteristics and historical relationship variables available before the outcome was observed.

The model was designed primarily as a **ranking and segmentation tool**, rather than as a causal model.

---

## 3. Population and Target Definition

The initial population consisted of:

**1,539,498 customers who had previously abandoned the institution.**

Customers were evaluated based on whether they returned to an active relationship during the observation period.

The target variable was defined as:

| Target | Definition                          |
| ------ | ----------------------------------- |
| `1`    | Customer returned / was reactivated |
| `0`    | Customer remained inactive          |

The modeling framework therefore treated winback as a binary classification problem.

---

## 4. Data Preparation

The modeling dataset was constructed from historical customer information.

Several variables describing the customer's relationship with the institution were considered, including:

* Recency / months inactive
* Savings relationship
* Checking account relationship
* Employment seniority
* Customer seniority
* Age
* Credit score
* Number of credit-related interactions
* Number of investments / term deposits
* Geographic or commercial location
* Other customer relationship characteristics

The analysis included data preparation, variable transformation and treatment of categorical and numerical variables before model development.

---

## 5. Weight of Evidence (WOE)

Weight of Evidence (WOE) transformation was used to analyze the relationship between explanatory variables and the winback outcome.

For a given variable bin:

$$
WOE_i =
\ln \left(
\frac{\text{\\% good}_i}{\text{\\% bad}_i}
\right)
$$

WOE analysis provided two main benefits:

1. Understanding the relationship between predictors and winback.
2. Transforming variables into a form suitable for scorecard-style logistic regression modeling.

Information Value (IV) was also considered as part of variable analysis and selection.

---

## 6. Modeling Approach

The main predictive approach was **logistic regression**.

The model estimates the probability of customer winback as:

$$
P(Y=1|X)=
\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\cdots+\beta_kX_k)}}
$$

The modeling process included:

1. Data preparation
2. Exploratory analysis
3. WOE transformation
4. Variable evaluation
5. Logistic regression
6. Train/test validation
7. Probability estimation
8. Model discrimination analysis

The final model was designed to generate an estimated probability of winback for each customer.

---

## 7. Model Validation

Model performance was evaluated using several complementary metrics:

* ROC curve
* AUC
* KS statistic
* Gini coefficient
* Confusion matrix
* Lift and Gain analysis
* DeLong's test for comparison of correlated ROC curves

### ROC / AUC

The evaluated model specifications achieved an AUC of approximately 0.8364, indicating strong discrimination between customers who subsequently returned and those who remained inactive.

The full and reduced models showed virtually identical ROC performance:

| Model                     | Predictors |          AUC |         KS |
| ------------------------- | ---------: | -----------: | ---------: |
| Full model                |         11 |     0.836444 |     0.5232 |
| **Reduced model — Final** |      **9** | **0.836372** | **0.5230** |

The ROC curves of both specifications were nearly indistinguishable, suggesting that the additional predictors included in the full model provided very limited incremental discrimination.

### Model Comparison

Several model specifications were evaluated to assess the trade-off between model complexity and predictive performance.

The full model included the complete set of candidate predictors, while the reduced model retained a smaller subset of variables.

The two models were compared using DeLong's test for correlated ROC curves, since both models were evaluated on the same test observations.

The results were:

* Full model AUC: **0.836444**
* Reduced model AUC: **0.836372**
* Absolute AUC difference: **0.000072**
* DeLong Z: **2.7628**
* p-value: **0.00573**

The DeLong test indicates a statistically significant difference between the two AUCs. However, the absolute difference in AUC is extremely small.

This distinction is important: statistical significance does not necessarily imply meaningful incremental predictive value. In this case, the full model provides only a negligible improvement in discrimination while requiring additional predictors.

The reduced model therefore represents a more parsimonious specification with virtually identical predictive performance.

From a practical modeling perspective, this illustrates the importance of evaluating not only whether a statistical difference exists, but also whether the magnitude of that difference justifies the additional model complexity.

---

## 8. Organic Winback Results

After the model was developed, the institution observed the natural reactivation behavior of the inactive customer population.

By December 2022:

* Initial inactive population: **1,539,498 customers**
* Customers classified as activable: **194,307**
* Activable population as a percentage of the original inactive population: **12.62%**
* Observed reactivation rate among activable customers: **44.02%**

The model therefore concentrated customers with a substantially higher observed propensity to reactivate within a relatively small segment of the inactive population.

The **44.02% reactivation rate** represents observed organic customer behavior rather than an incremental effect generated by a campaign.

Because customers classified as activable did not receive a specific intervention as a consequence of the model, this result should not be interpreted as a causal estimate of campaign effectiveness.

---

## 9. Model Interpretation

The model should be interpreted as a **propensity model**.

It answers:

> **Which inactive customers are more likely to return organically?**

It does not answer:

> **Which customers will return because we contact them?**

This distinction is fundamental to interpreting the business results.

The model successfully identified a segment with a higher concentration of customers who subsequently returned to an active relationship. However, these reactivations occurred organically, without a targeted intervention driven by the model. Therefore, the observed reactivation rate does not establish that the model, or any intervention based on its predictions, caused those customers to return.

The observed organic reactivation rate can instead be interpreted as a **baseline level of natural customer reactivation**. The model provides a mechanism for identifying customers whose likelihood of reactivation is higher than that of the broader inactive population.

This creates an opportunity to move from **prediction to action**. By applying targeted strategies to customers identified as having a high propensity to reactivate, the institution could potentially generate additional reactivations and improve the overall activation conversion rate relative to the natural baseline.

The incremental impact of such actions, however, would need to be validated through a controlled experiment, such as an A/B test or randomized treatment-control design.

---

## 10. Business Value

The model provides a mechanism for prioritizing a large inactive customer population.

Rather than treating all 1.5+ million inactive customers equally, the model identifies a substantially smaller segment with a higher observed propensity to reactivate. This allows resources to be concentrated on customers who already exhibit stronger signals of potential re-engagement.

Potential applications include:

* Prioritization of future winback campaigns
* Customer segmentation
* Contact prioritization
* Resource allocation
* Estimation of potential reactivation opportunities
* Identification of customer characteristics associated with reactivation

The key business opportunity is not simply to identify customers who are likely to return naturally, but to use that information to **design more efficient interventions**. If targeted actions are applied to the high-propensity segment, the institution can test whether the observed organic reactivation rate can be increased further, generating **incremental activation lift**.

For example, the model could be used to establish a prioritized campaign population, while a control group is retained to measure the natural reactivation baseline. The difference in activation between the treatment and control groups would provide an estimate of the incremental effect generated by the intervention.

The comparison between the full and reduced specifications also demonstrates that predictive performance can be maintained with a more parsimonious model.

A future implementation could therefore use the predicted probability as a ranking variable and establish operational cutoffs according to campaign capacity, expected economic value, and available customer-contact resources.

In this framework, the model becomes more than a predictive tool: it serves as the **targeting layer for a measurable winback strategy**, where the ultimate objective is not merely to predict reactivation, but to determine whether targeted actions can increase activation beyond the natural reactivation baseline.

---

## 11. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

### No Controlled Intervention

Customers classified as activable did not receive a treatment specifically triggered by the model.

Therefore, the analysis does not estimate the causal effectiveness of a winback campaign.

### Organic Reactivation

The observed outcome reflects natural customer behavior and potentially other factors outside the model.

The 44.02% reactivation rate should therefore be interpreted as an observed concentration of naturally returning customers rather than incremental lift generated by the model.

### Temporal Considerations

The model was developed using information available before the winback observation period. Future implementations should preserve this temporal structure to avoid data leakage.

---

## 12. Future Improvements

A natural next step would be to move from **propensity modeling** to **incremental response modeling**.

A controlled experiment could randomly assign customers to:

* **Treatment:** receive a winback intervention
* **Control:** receive no intervention

The incremental effect could then be estimated as:

$$
\Delta =
P(Y=1|Treatment)-P(Y=1|Control)
$$

This would allow the institution to distinguish between customers who are likely to return regardless of intervention and customers whose behavior can actually be influenced by a winback strategy.

This would enable the development of an **uplift model**.

Instead of predicting:

> **"Who is likely to return?"**

the model would aim to answer:

> **"Who is most likely to return because of the intervention?"**

This represents a natural evolution from predictive propensity modeling toward causal decision modeling.

## 13. Key Takeaways

- Built a predictive model to estimate customer winback propensity.
- Analyzed a population of **1,539,498 previously inactive customers**.
- Used **Weight of Evidence (WOE)** transformation and logistic regression.
- Evaluated model discrimination using **AUC, KS, Gini, ROC, Lift and Gain analysis**.
- Achieved an AUC of approximately **0.836**.
- Compared alternative model specifications using **DeLong's test**.
- Found virtually identical predictive performance between the full and reduced models.
- The full model achieved an AUC of **0.836444**, compared with **0.836372** for the reduced model.
- Although the AUC difference was statistically significant, the absolute improvement was only **0.000072**.
- The reduced model therefore provides a more parsimonious specification with virtually no loss in discrimination.
- Identified **194,307 customers (12.62% of the inactive population)** as activable.
- Observed a **44.02% organic reactivation rate** within the activable segment.
- Distinguished **predictive association from causal impact**.
- Identified controlled experimentation and **uplift modeling** as natural next steps.
